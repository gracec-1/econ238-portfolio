# Before We Panic About Data Centers, Let’s Ask Better Questions

A new data center could use hundreds of millions of gallons of water. It could require as much electricity as a small city and generate thousands of tons of waste. It could also bring thousands of cars onto local roads.

Those numbers sound alarming. And they should get our attention. But a big number alone does not tell us whether a project is actually harmful to a community.

The first question we should ask is: Compared to what?

Large institutions already use enormous amounts of resources. The University of Rochester and UR Medicine, for example, operate more than 15 million square feet of buildings across more than 700 acres. Their operations use about 225 million kilowatt hours of electricity and 2.6 million dekatherms of natural gas each year. They also use about 250 million gallons of domestic water annually.

That does not mean those impacts should be ignored. It means we need context before deciding what they mean.

The same principle applies to data centers. If a headline says a facility will use 800,000 gallons of water a day, we should ask how much water the facility actually consumes, where that water comes from, whether the supply is limited, and what other users would need that water. A permitted maximum should not automatically be treated as the amount actually used.

Electricity is similar. A facility using enormous amounts of electricity could put pressure on the power grid and potentially affect other customers. But we also need to know whether the company is paying for the electricity and infrastructure it requires, whether it is helping pay for new power capacity, and whether it is creating jobs, tax revenue, or other benefits for the community.

This is where the biggest concern becomes who pays and who benefits.

If a data center creates pollution, traffic, noise, or pressure on local water supplies, those costs may fall on nearby residents instead of the company creating them. That is a real problem. A project should not be allowed to make money by pushing its costs onto everyone else.

But the opposite mistake is also possible. We should not assume that every resource used by a large facility is automatically “wasted.” Data centers can provide jobs, investment, tax revenue and computing services that businesses and consumers value.

There is also a question of what Rochester gives up by choosing one project over another. Land, electricity, water and infrastructure are limited. If a data center uses those resources, we should consider what else could have used them.

So what should Rochester do?

We should neither blindly welcome data centers nor automatically reject them. Instead, communities should require clear information about their actual resource use and make companies account for the costs they impose. If a project needs expensive new infrastructure, the developer should not automatically expect taxpayers or other customers to pay for it. If it creates pollution or strains scarce resources, those costs should be part of the decision.

The next time you see a headline about a data center using “enough water for thousands of homes” or “as much electricity as a city,” don't ignore it, but don't stop there either.

Ask: Compared to what? Who benefits? Who pays? And what are we giving up to make it happen?

Those questions won't make the decision simple. But they can help Rochester make a decision based on the full picture rather than the scariest number in the headline.
